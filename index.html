Overview

The charterer lost about $1.06 million net across 420 calls

Main finding. Demurrage happens about as often everywhere: close to half of the calls in every terminal and vessel class. What changes is how much it costs when it happens. Large vessels carry most of the cost, and Panamax/Kamsarmax calls at the multi-bulk terminal alone account for 47% of the net loss from 8% of the calls.

Data and method

How each call's result is worked out

Six datasets were joined: port calls (timestamps, vessel, cargo), laytime terms (notice of readiness, handling rate, demurrage and despatch rates), delay events, a 20-call benchmark, terminal reference ranges and vessel-class ranges.

Laytime rules applied to every call

  1. Laytime starts at the earlier of notice expiry (NOR + notice period) and the start of cargo work.
  2. Allowed laytime is cargo t ÷ rate t/day × 24 hours.
  3. Counted laytime runs from laytime start to completion of cargo work, minus delay events that do not count toward laytime.
  4. Over the allowance: the charterer pays demurrage at the contract rate per day, pro rata.
  5. Under the allowance: the owner pays despatch at 50% of the demurrage rate per day saved.

Data validation: 11 of 11 checks passed

  • 420 calls and 420 terms rows
  • Call IDs unique and matching
  • Every delay event links to a call
  • No missing values
  • Delay flag is only yes or no
  • Timestamps in order, arrival to departure
  • Event durations equal end − start
  • DWT and cargo inside vessel-class ranges
  • Handling rate inside terminal range
  • Commodity supported by terminal
  • Despatch rate is 50% of demurrage rate

The recalculated laytime start and allowed laytime also matched the values supplied in the terms file for all 420 calls.

Insights

What the results suggest

  1. Severity, not frequency, drives the cost

    About 49.5% of calls end in demurrage in every terminal and vessel class. The worst 10% of calls make up 61% of all demurrage charged, so controlling the few long overruns matters more than the average call.

  2. Large vessels at the multi-bulk terminal are the priority

    Panamax/Kamsarmax calls at the multi-bulk terminal have a median result of −$23,326, so more than half of them lost over $23k each. These 35 calls produce 47% of the net loss.

  3. Negotiate laytime before rates

    Changing allowed laytime by ±10% moves the total by about −$1.33M to +$1.08M. Changing the demurrage rate by ±10% moves it by about ±$106k. A laytime allowance about 9.7% higher, or about 6.6 extra hours per call, would bring the charterer to break-even.

  4. The despatch clause was worth about $203k across these calls

    Without it, the net loss rises to $1.26M. It partly offsets demurrage but cannot cover it, because despatch is paid at half the rate.

Limitations

What these results cannot tell you

  • Small groups. Terminal × class cells hold 35 to 70 calls. Their means are indicative, and their tails (P5, P95, worst case) are not reliable.
  • Commodity is tied to terminal and vessel class. Each commodity appears at one terminal on two vessel classes, so its effect cannot be isolated.
  • A balanced dataset. The near-identical demurrage share in every group suggests the data was built to be balanced. How often demurrage occurs should not be generalised to other fleets or ports.
  • One lever at a time. The sensitivity charts move one term while holding the rest fixed, and combined changes are only explored in the what-if model.
  • Behaviour held fixed. The model assumes vessels would use the same time under different terms. In practice, more generous laytime can lead to slower operations.
  • Simplified laytime counting. Excluded delay events are subtracted as supplied. Clauses such as "once on demurrage, always on demurrage", weather working days or shifting time are not modelled separately.
  • One year, two terminals. 420 calls during 2024 at two terminals.
